Why Christmas Light Installs Book Out Before Thanksgiving

Every year I get calls the first week of December from people ready to book Christmas lights, and most years I am already telling them no. Not because we do not want the work. Because by the first of December the install calendar is spoken for, and whatever is left is the slot that weather has already shoved around twice. The install season in East Texas is a lot shorter than it looks on a calendar, and nearly every decision about it gets made in the first two weeks of November.

People treat a light install like getting on the books for a mow. It is closer to a harvest. The window is narrow, the weather inside it is unreliable, and when it closes it does not reopen.

The install window here is about five weeks long.

Almost nobody in Longview wants lights burning before Halloween is over, and almost nobody wants them going up after the second week of December, because by then you have already lost half the nights you were paying to enjoy. That leaves roughly the first weekend of November through December 10. Call it five weeks, and about twenty-five usable workdays once you take out Sundays and Thanksgiving.

A full roofline on a single-story ranch is most of a morning. A two-story with multiple gables, dormers, and a wrapped porch is a full day for a crew, sometimes more if the peaks need anchors set. Then add the houses that want wreaths, porch columns, tree wraps, and a timer schedule that actually matches when the family is home. Twenty-five days does not stretch very far against that.

Materials are the other half of it. Bulbs, wire, sockets, and clips get ordered in volume ahead of the season, because every run is cut to a measured roofline. A December caller is not just asking for a day on the schedule. They are asking for material committed in October.

What to do instead: get on the calendar in October or the first week of November, even if the date you take feels absurdly early. Lights on a timer do not have to come on until you say so. Being installed on November 10 and dark until Thanksgiving night costs you nothing, and it is the only version of this where you actually get the date you want.

November weather decides the schedule, not the calendar.

Longview averages around four and a half inches of rain in November, and it does not arrive politely. We get fronts stacked up every five to seven days, and a single three-day rain event in the middle of the month pushes every job behind it into the week before Thanksgiving.

Nobody from my crew goes onto a composition shingle roof that is wet, and nobody goes up on one that still has frost on it. By mid-November, frost on a roof is a normal Gregg County morning. A south-facing slope dries by nine. A north-facing slope shaded by a pine can stay slick until almost eleven, so a crew can sit in a driveway on a clear, dry-looking day with nothing to do yet.

The other squeeze is daylight. Daylight saving time ended on November 1, so sunset in Longview is a little after 5:20 and the useful light is gone before 5:30. On a good November day we get maybe five or six solid hours of roof time. On a wet one we get zero. That is the real reason the calendar fills in the first half of the month: the early bookings are the only ones with any slack left to absorb a rain week.

The pines make it worse in a way people out here will recognize. Loblolly needles drop heavy through November, pile in the valleys, and a layer of wet needles on a shingle slope behaves like ball bearings. On a lot of properties we blow the roof off before we can clip anything to it.

A box of store-bought lights is not the same product.

What we hang is commercial-grade C9: individual sockets spaced a foot apart on heavier SPT wire, cut to the measured length of your run and crimped with an end cap. No bunched slack at the corner of the garage, no dark two-foot gap where the strand ran out, no extension cord taped along the fascia. The line follows the roof because it was built for that roof.

A big-box strand is a fixed twenty-five feet with molded plugs on light-duty wire, and the instructions will tell you not to run more than three to five sets end to end. Rooflines do not come in twenty-five-foot increments, so you either stop short or you daisy-chain past the rating, which is where the GFCI starts tripping at six in the evening when the oven is on. When one of those sets fails at the midpoint, you are not replacing a bulb. You are replacing the set.

The rest of what you are paying for is less visible. All-weather clips sized to your shingle or gutter, so nothing gets stapled or nailed into fascia. Load spread across separate circuits instead of everything on one outdoor outlet. A timer per circuit, set to your schedule. And a phone number, so when a run goes dark the week before Christmas somebody comes out and fixes it instead of you deciding to live with it.

What to do instead: if you are going to do it yourself, do it with the right material. Buy C9 sockets on SPT wire by the spool with separate bulbs and clips, measure every roofline and eave before you order, and plan your circuits before you plug anything in. It costs more the first year and less every year after.

A two-story roofline in the cold is the wrong place to learn.

This is the part I will be blunt about. A two-story roofline in November means a ladder footed on wet clay or pine straw, a cold slick slope, hands that have stopped working right, and the particular hurry that sets in when you can see the light going. Ladder falls climb every year in November and December, and the ones I hear about around here are never from the porch. They are from the second story, late in the afternoon, on the last run.

Steep pitch makes it a different job entirely. Past about an 8/12 you are not walking it, you are working off anchors and a rope. That is equipment and practice, not nerve.

What to do instead: split the job. Hire out the two-story runs and the high peaks, and do the shrubs, porch, and walkway yourself if you enjoy it. Some of the smallest tickets we write are exactly that split, and they are a good use of money.

Takedown and storage in January is half of what you are buying.

We pull lights in the first two or three weeks of January, coil each run, label it to the roofline it came off of, and store it. That last part is why year two goes faster and prices better: your house already has a cut set of runs that fit it.

Lights that stay up past January do not just look rough. Our sun fades bulbs and chalks wire by spring, the clips go brittle and start snapping instead of releasing, and squirrels work on exposed wire all winter. Sets that come down in January and sit in a box outlast sets that live on the roof by years.

We install, service, and take down Christmas lights across Longview, Hallsville, White Oak, Kilgore, and Gladewater, and November is the month that decides whether you get a date or a wait list. If you want lights on before Thanksgiving, this is the week to call.
