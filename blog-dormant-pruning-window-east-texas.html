Winter Is When Tree Work Should Actually Happen Here

Almost every call I get about a tree comes in July, usually right after a limb lands on something expensive. I understand why. But the right month to cut most of our shade trees is December or January, and on oaks in particular, waiting until spring is a mistake you cannot take back.

December and January are the window, and it is not about our convenience.

By early December our hardwoods have dropped their leaves and gone fully dormant. Nothing is moving.

Cut a big limb in September and the wound sits open while the tree winds down for the year. Cut it in late December and the tree is two months from its spring flush, which is exactly when it builds the callus tissue that closes a wound. You are timing the injury right before the repair crew shows up, and the insects and fungi that colonize fresh cuts are dormant too.

Pines are the exception worth naming, since so much of our canopy is loblolly. They do not need structural pruning the way a water oak does, and fresh resin in warm weather calls in Ips beetles. A pine limb comes off in the cold.

What to do instead: put structural pruning on the calendar between the first hard freeze and the end of January. Save the summer calls for genuine hazards: a cracked limb over the driveway, a storm hanger, something grinding on the roof.

Oaks have a hard deadline, and February is the wrong side of it.

This part I will not soften. Oak wilt is a fungal disease that moves through East Texas in pockets and kills red oaks fast. Water oak, Shumard, southern red oak: a red oak that gets it is often dead inside a season.

Sap-feeding beetles no bigger than a grain of rice carry the spores, and they are drawn to fresh wounds. Texas A&M Forest Service has said the same thing for years: do not prune oaks between February 1 and June 30. That is when the beetles are flying and fungal mats on infected red oaks are pushing spores.

The oak window is July through January, and the dormant end is the best part.

What to do instead: prune oaks in the dormant window, and paint every oak wound immediately, in every month of the year. A spray of cheap latex paint over the fresh wood within a few minutes of the cut. Oaks are the only tree I paint. If a limb tears off one in April, paint it that day and leave the real work until July.

A bare canopy shows you everything the leaves were hiding.

Stand under your biggest shade tree in January and look straight up. You can see the whole architecture. In July you cannot see a third of it.

Deadwood is the obvious find. In a leafless canopy a dead limb has no buds at the tips, loose or missing bark, and a gray cast against the live wood. In summer it is buried in the green around it, and most of the deadwood I take out, the homeowner never knew was there.

Included bark is the one that matters most and gets noticed least. Where two stems meet at a narrow V instead of a wide U, bark gets pinched inside the union instead of the stems growing wood together. That seam is not attached, and it fails in an ice storm or a hard wind, usually taking half the tree. Water oaks and Bradford pears are notorious for it, and winter is the only time you can read those unions from the ground.

Crossing limbs are the third. Two limbs grinding on each other wear through the bark and hold a wound open as long as the rubbing continues. Leafless, you can pick the right one to take instead of guessing.

What to do instead: walk a lap around each big tree in January with your phone and photograph anything dead, any narrow V union, anything crossing. Fifteen minutes gives you a list instead of a vague feeling that the tree needs something.

Some of these cuts are yours and some of them are not.

Plenty of winter pruning is a homeowner job. My line is simple: both feet on the ground, both hands on the saw, nothing thicker than two inches.

That covers suckers off the base, water sprouts off a lateral, a low limb in the walkway, and small deadwood you can reach with a pole pruner. Cut just outside the branch collar, the swollen ring where the limb meets the trunk, and never flush. The collar is what seals the wound. On anything heavier than a wrist, use three cuts: undercut a foot out, top cut just past it, then the finish cut at the collar. Skip that and the limb peels bark down the trunk on its way off.

Two more rules. Take no more than about a quarter of the live canopy in a season, and never top a tree, crepe myrtles included, which I have written a whole post about.

What is not a homeowner job: anything needing a ladder, anything within ten feet of a power line, anything over the roof, any structural cut on a limb thicker than your arm, and any tree with conks or cavities. A bad cut cannot be undone on a forty-year-old tree.

What to do instead: for the big structural work, bring in an ISA certified arborist and ask for the certification number and a current certificate of insurance before anybody climbs. Somebody knocking on your door offering to top your oaks for cash is not that.

Wet winter clay decides when equipment can reach the tree.

Here is the East Texas wrinkle the national pruning articles leave out. Our winters are not dry. Fronts come through on a rhythm, the sun sits low, evaporation is almost nothing, and our clay holds water for days. A yard that drains fine in August can stay soft for a week in January.

Getting to the tree means getting equipment to it, and a loaded chipper truck on saturated clay does two kinds of damage. The visible kind is ruts and torn turf that will not fill in until spring green-up. The worse kind is compaction in the root zone, which is close to permanent.

So winter scheduling here is weather scheduling. We watch for a dry stretch, stage the chipper on the driveway, drag brush out to the equipment instead of driving in to it, and lay plywood when a machine has to cross turf. On a soft week the right answer is to move the job.

What to do instead: book dormant pruning early in the window rather than late. December and the first half of January leave room to slide around a wet front and still finish before February 1. The last week of January leaves no slack at all.

We handle dormant pruning, deadwood removal, and structural work on shade trees across Longview, Hallsville, White Oak, Kilgore, and Gladewater, and this is the stretch of the year when it should be happening. If you have an oak on your mind, that window closes February 1.
