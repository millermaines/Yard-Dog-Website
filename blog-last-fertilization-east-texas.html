The Last Feeding of the Year, and Why It Is Not Nitrogen

Every October somebody asks me to put down one more round of fertilizer to green the lawn back up before winter. I understand the impulse. The lawn is fading, the weather finally feels good, and a bag of nitrogen is cheap. It is also the single easiest way to hurt a warm-season lawn in East Texas, and you will not see the damage until March.

Bermuda and St. Augustine do not spend October growing. They spend it moving sugars and starches down into rhizomes, stolons, and roots so there is something in the tank when they break dormancy in April. That storage process is the whole point of fall for a warm-season grass, and it is quiet work. Nothing about it looks impressive from the street.

Nitrogen interrupts it. Nitrogen tells the plant to build new leaf tissue right now, and leaf tissue is expensive. The sugars that would have gone into storage go into soft new blades instead, and the plant walks into winter with a thinner reserve and a canopy full of growth that has not had time to harden off.

Our first freeze is close enough that late nitrogen is a real gamble.

Gregg County usually takes its first freeze somewhere in the second week of November, give or take a couple of weeks in either direction. I have seen it land October 31 and I have seen Thanksgiving come and go without one. That is the problem: you are fertilizing against a date nobody knows.

Grass that gets a shot of nitrogen in late October is pushing tender, high-moisture tissue for the next three or four weeks. Tissue that young has not accumulated the sugars and cell-wall changes that let a plant survive ice forming in it. When the freeze does come, that growth dies back hard, and on St. Augustine the damage can reach past the leaf and into the stolon, which is where next year's lawn lives.

The second half of the bill shows up later. Lush, nitrogen-rich tissue sitting in a cool, wet canopy is exactly what large patch wants, and large patch is a fall and spring disease here, not a summer one. Fertilize heavy in late October and a lot of what you see in March looks like winterkill but is actually disease that got invited in the previous fall.

What to do instead: treat your last nitrogen application of the year as a late-summer job, not a fall job. In this part of East Texas that means the first week of September for St. Augustine and mid-September for bermuda, with the whole point being to let the grass use it and harden off before temperatures drop. After that, nitrogen does more harm than good.

Potassium is the only nutrient that genuinely helps a lawn go into winter.

Potassium does not make grass grow. It regulates water movement inside the plant, stiffens cell walls, and keeps the stomates working the way they should. In practice that means a lawn with adequate potassium handles cold, drought, and disease pressure better than the same lawn without it, and it goes into dormancy holding more of its root reserve.

It is not a green-up product and will not make the lawn look better in November. It makes the lawn more likely to come out of March with a full stand instead of thin spots you have to repair.

If you do apply it, the products are straightforward. Potassium sulfate, labeled 0-0-50, is the one I prefer because it carries sulfur and is easier on the lawn than muriate of potash. Potassium chloride, labeled 0-0-60, is cheaper and works fine on established turf. Rate is about one pound of actual potash per thousand square feet, which is roughly two pounds of 0-0-50 product per thousand. Water it in. The right window here is late September into the first half of October, when the grass still has root activity to take it up.

What to do instead: if you want a last feeding, make it straight potassium and get it down by mid-October, not late October. And do not buy a bag labeled "winterizer" without reading the analysis. Most winterizer blends on the shelf are formulated for cool-season lawns up north and lead with nitrogen, which is the opposite of what bermuda or St. Augustine needs here.

A soil test will usually tell you to skip it.

Here is the part that costs a fertilizer company money to say out loud: a lot of East Texas clay already holds plenty of potassium. Clay has real cation exchange capacity, meaning it grips and holds nutrients instead of letting them wash through the way sand does. Plenty of the samples I see out here come back adequate or high on potassium and short on something else entirely, usually nitrogen management or pH.

So applying potassium blind is a coin flip, and excess potassium can interfere with magnesium and calcium uptake.

A soil test through the Texas A&M AgriLife Extension soil testing lab runs about the price of one bag of fertilizer. The Gregg County Extension office has the forms and the mailing boxes. Pull ten to fifteen plugs from across the lawn at four to six inches deep, mix them in a clean bucket, and send a cup of that.

Late October is still a good time to pull one. Results come back in a couple of weeks, which puts a real plan in your hands before spring, not after it starts.

What to do instead: test before you buy anything. If potassium comes back adequate, your money is better spent on a lime application if pH is low, which it often is under pines out here, or on core aeration next summer to open up compacted clay.

After the third week of October, the feeding year is over.

That is the cutoff I use, and it is not arbitrary. Soil temperatures at four inches in Gregg County are usually sliding through the low 60s by the last week of October, and a warm-season grass simply is not taking up much of anything below that. Whatever you spread after that point is sitting on the soil waiting on a rain to carry it into the storm drain.

If you are reading this the week it went up, you are past both windows. Nitrogen closed in September. Potassium closed around the middle of this month. The right move now is to stop.

Letting a lawn go into dormancy clean is not neglect. Keep mowing at your normal height until growth genuinely stops, usually somewhere in November, so leaf litter does not build up in the canopy. Keep leaves off the grass, particularly St. Augustine. Give the lawn a deep watering before the first hard freeze if we are dry, because moist soil holds heat better than dry soil and protects crowns and roots. Then leave it alone until green-up.

The lawn that looks best next May will not be the one that got a bonus feeding in late October. It will be the one that was fed correctly in June, left alone in November, and hit with pre-emergent and nitrogen on time in the spring.
