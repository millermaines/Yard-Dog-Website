What we do

Fertilization isn't about putting down more. It's about putting it down the week the grass can use it. We start with a soil test, then build a feeding schedule around your grass type and the East Texas season, not the back of a bag written for somewhere up north.

A full program is four visits. Slow-release nitrogen goes down in late April once nights hold above 60 degrees. A lighter, summer-safe feeding follows in early July so the lawn doesn't run on empty through August. The last real nitrogen goes down in early September to push roots before dormancy, and a low-nitrogen, potassium-heavy winterizer goes down in October for cold hardiness. If the test shows low pH, which is common here, we correct it with lime. Core aeration fits in late summer on compacted lawns.

How the job goes

The first feeding goes down around April 25, once overnight lows hold above 60 and the grass is fully awake. Feed in March and most of it washes out with the next rain, because bermuda and St. Augustine are still half-dormant and can't pull it in. The last nitrogen goes down in the first two weeks of September, four to six weeks ahead of Longview's mid-November first freeze.

  1. Walk the lawn and test the soil

    We look at the grass type, the shade and the thin spots, and pull a soil sample so the plan is built on numbers.

  2. Build the schedule

    You get a written plan showing what goes down, when and why, from a single soil-correction visit up to the full four-step program.

  3. Feed in the window

    Each application goes down the week the grass can use it, coordinated with your mowing schedule.

  4. Aerate where it's packed

    Compacted lawns get core aeration between late August and mid-September, with the feeding right behind it.

  5. Winterize and adjust

    October gets the potassium-heavy winterizer, and next year's plan adjusts to what the lawn and the soil test showed.

The bag on the shelf was written for somebody else's lawn

Big-box stores stack fertilizer out front the first week it hits 60 degrees, and they stack winterizer by the door in October. Both schedules were written for cool-season lawns up north. Soil in Gregg and Harrison counties doesn't reach 65 degrees until mid-April, and a heavy shot of nitrogen in mid-October pushes soft growth right before frost burns it off.

On St. Augustine, late nitrogen is worse than wasted. Large patch wakes up when nights drop into the 60s and the grass stays damp overnight, which is October here, and late feeding fuels it. That is why our last real nitrogen goes down in early September and October is a potassium month. Years of pine needles and acidic rain also push most East Texas soil a little low on pH, so we test and add lime when it's needed.

When should I fertilize my lawn in East Texas?

The spring window opens around April 25, once nights hold above 60 degrees, and runs through May. After that, keep the tank from running dry with a lighter feeding in early July and a fall feeding in early September. Skip March, when the grass is still half-dormant, and skip heavy nitrogen in October, when it only pushes growth the frost will burn off.

Does your fertilization program include weed control?

No. We are not a spray outfit, and we don't apply weed, insect or disease products of any kind. Our program is fertilizer, soil testing, lime when the test calls for it, core aeration and the fall winterizer. If you want a lawn sprayed, that is a job for a pest control company. What we do is keep the grass itself fed and well rooted.

Do you aerate lawns?

Yes. We core aerate, which pulls plugs out of the ground instead of poking holes, because a spike aerator just shoves clay sideways and packs it tighter. The window here is late August through mid-September, while the grass can still heal the holes. The lawn should be watered the day before so the tines don't skip on dry clay, and the plugs stay where they fall.

Do bermuda and St. Augustine need a winterizer?

Not the high-nitrogen kind sold for cool-season lawns. Our grasses slow down in fall instead of growing into December, so late nitrogen just pushes soft growth the frost burns off, and on St. Augustine it can feed large patch. What helps is a low-nitrogen, potassium-heavy feeding in October for rooting and cold hardiness, if the soil test says the lawn needs it.
