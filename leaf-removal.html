What we do

Leaf removal here is not one Saturday with a rake. Pecans drop first, sweetgums let go in early November, and post oaks and water oaks hang on to dead leaves and drop them a few at a time into February. We time cleanups to what is actually falling on your lot, not to a date on the calendar.

Here is how a visit works. The crew blows leaves out of the lawn, the shrub beds and the rock beds onto the driveway or another paved surface. We pull the vacuum trailer up to that spot and vacuum everything up. The vacuum shreds as it goes, roughly 10 yards of leaves down to 1, so one trailer hauls off what would otherwise be a curb full of bags. Nothing gets blown into the neighbor's yard or the storm drain, and we re-edge the bed lines the leaves buried before we leave.

Because nothing gets bagged, you aren't paying for bags or for the time it takes to fill them. Leaf removal is straight labor, billed by the hour, and we tell you how many hours we expect before we start. On smaller lots the light early drop can go back into the lawn through the mower, which feeds the soil instead of filling the trailer.

St. Augustine is the grass that pays for wet leaves

Bermuda goes dormant early and grows from underground rhizomes, so leaves on a bermuda lawn in December are mostly just ugly. St. Augustine is different. It grows from runners on the surface and stays partly green into November and sometimes December, so every growing point sits right where the leaves land.

Bury that under a wet leaf mat with soil in the 60s and you have the setup for large patch, the cool-season face of brown patch. In March the homeowner finds dead circles and calls it winterkill. Often it was the leaves, not the cold. If you have St. Augustine under pecans, sweetgums or post oaks, count November leaf cover as urgent, not cosmetic.

When should leaves be removed in East Texas?

Start collecting in the first half of November, when the sweetgums drop and a mower can't keep up anymore. Plan on another pass in mid-December. Post oaks and water oaks hold dead leaves all winter and let them go into February, so a final cleanup before spring green-up is worth it. The light October drop can just be mulched in.

Can I just mulch my leaves with the mower?

Early in the season, yes. The first light drop in October can go back into the lawn with two passes of the mower while the leaves are dry. The test is simple: if you can still see green grass when you're done, you did it right. If it reads brown, you put a blanket on the lawn and it's time to pick them up.

Is it OK to leave leaves in my flower beds?

A few inches of loose leaves on a bed of established shrubs is fine, but it doesn't replace mulch. Whole leaves mat into shingled layers after a good rain and shed water sideways, and they blow back onto the lawn by February. Keep them off perennial crowns and shrub stems, and don't bury pansies or other winter color.

Do you bag the leaves?

No. We vacuum them. The crew blows the leaves onto your driveway or another paved surface, and we vacuum them into our trailer and haul them off. The vacuum shreds them about 10 to 1, so roughly 10 yards of leaves leaves your property as 1 yard. We don't blow leaves into the street, the storm drain or the neighbor's yard. Pine straw is the one exception: it makes good mulch, so if you want it kept in the beds, we leave it there and only clear it off the lawn.

Do I need a driveway for the leaf vacuum?

It helps. We blow the leaves onto a driveway or another paved surface where the trailer can pull up. On the walkthrough we look at where the trailer can reach and plan the job around it, and that is part of the hour estimate we give you.
